SEVERITY ANNOTATION GUIDE
=========================

You are comparing a REFERENCE (the true, human-verified transcript) to
a HYPOTHESIS (what the speech-to-text system produced). For each pair,
give a severity score from 0 to 4 describing how much the ASR error(s)
changed the meaning of the sentence. Base your judgement only on the
reference-hypothesis pair given to you -- do not assume outside context
that isn't in the sentence.


SEVERITY SCALE
--------------

0 - No meaning change.
Only the surface changed: casing, punctuation, filler words, dialect
spelling, or a spelling variant of a name that's still clearly
recognizable as the same name.

1 - Trivial error.
Words are garbled or reworded, but you can tell exactly what was meant
just from this sentence -- no outside knowledge needed. This includes
nonsense words that don't match any real alternative (example: "Japer"
for "Jaipur"). Even if the words used are real words, if they don't add
up to a believable different meaning (example: "seeing her goggle"),
treat it the same as nonsense -- still a 1.
IMPORTANT: if you are not fully confident what was meant, it is NOT a
1. Move to 2 instead.

2 - Ambiguous change.
You need to think about it, guess, or you're left unsure what was
meant. This also covers small linking words changing (example: "in"
becoming "and") in a way that changes how two things relate to each
other, not just what they individually are. It also applies when the
same word gets garbled differently more than once in one sentence --
don't assume it's fine just because you can eventually guess the
answer; if it took real work to get there, it's a 2. Also covers total,
unrecoverable loss of a detail (the content is just gone, not just
unclear) -- write "totally garbled" in your note. This always stays at
2 even when the lost detail was important, since 3 and 4 both require
a clear meaning change to actually be asserted, and garbling that bad
can't assert anything clearly.

3 - Clear factual error.
A real, different, believable thing was substituted in place of the
correct one -- a different real name, place, number, time, or action
(examples: "Joan" instead of "John," "Shannon" instead of "Schengen").
There is no real doubt about what is now being claimed -- it's simply a
different, wrong, but plausible fact.

4 - Critical change.
The core point of the sentence is reversed, invented, or completely
changed. This includes an obvious "not" being added or removed, but
ALSO includes smaller word swaps that flip the meaning without using
the word "not" at all (examples: "this" becoming "there," "I can't
believe you didn't" becoming "I can't even") -- including tense
changes that flip whether something happened, is happening, or hasn't
happened yet, when the swap is consequential and nothing else in the
sentence corrects it. Nothing in the sentence warns the reader that
this changed. Also covers made-up content filling in for a part the
human transcriber marked as inaudible or unclear (e.g. tagged as
[inc]) -- there is nothing to check that content against, so treat it
as critical (4) by default, not by normal recovery rules.

NOT SCORED AT ALL: if the reference is only a tag with no real words in
it at all (example: <OVERLAP> by itself, nothing else), skip it. There
is nothing to compare, so it doesn't get a 0-4 score.


ERROR TYPES
-----------

For each pair, also note the MAIN type of error present (pick one, even
if more than one small thing changed -- pick whichever one is driving
your severity score).

1. Acceptable variation
   The hypothesis differs from the reference only in ways that don't
   change meaning at all: capitalization, punctuation, filler words
   (um, eh), dialect spelling normalised to standard English, or a
   name spelled slightly differently but still clearly the same name.
   Usually severity 0.

2. Surface-form error
   Words are garbled, misheard, or reworded, but the meaning is still
   clearly recoverable just from this sentence. Usually a nonsense
   string standing in for a real word or name, with no real competing
   alternative meaning. Usually severity 0-1.

3. Content substitution
   A CONTENT-BEARING word is replaced by a different one -- this means
   things like names, numbers, times, places, amounts, or actions.
   This does NOT include small linking/connector words like "and," "or,"
   "in," "of," "on" -- those go under error type 4 below, even though
   they are also technically "substitutions." Usually severity 1-4,
   depending on whether the substitute is real/plausible (higher) or
   nonsense (lower).

4. Addition, omission, or structural error
   Words, clauses, or claims are added, dropped, reordered, or the
   relationship between two parts of the sentence is changed. This
   INCLUDES linking/connector word changes (and/or/in/of/on) that
   change how two things relate to each other (example: "leisure
   activities ON work time" becoming "leisure activities AND work
   time" -- changes one benefit into two vague ones). Usually severity
   1-4.

5. Polarity or modality error
   Negation, certainty, possibility, or TENSE is changed -- for example
   "did" becoming "did not," or "may have" becoming "did," or present
   tense becoming past tense in a way that changes whether something is
   ongoing, finished, or hadn't happened yet. This ALSO covers cases
   where the meaning flips without literally changing a negation word,
   through a different kind of word swap entirely (example: "this"
   becoming "there," changing what a sentence is actually claiming).
   WATCH FOR: a dialect word normalised to standard English in the
   WRONG tense (example: "dinnae" [present: don't] rendered as "didn't"
   [past]) -- this looks like the free, no-penalty dialect normalising
   under type 1 above, but it isn't, since the tense changed too, not
   just the spelling. Usually severity 1-4 depending on materiality,
   often 4 if the flip is complete and nothing in the sentence
   corrects it.


QUESTIONS TO THINK THROUGH (don't score these separately -- just reason
through them to help you land on one final severity score):

1. Materiality -- Does this error matter? Does it affect identity,
   action, object, time, place, amount, or the order of events?

2. Directionality -- Does the hypothesis just say the same thing
   differently, or does it actually change what happened, who did it,
   or whether it happened at all?

3. Recoverability -- Can you confidently tell what was meant from just
   this reference-hypothesis pair, without guessing or assuming extra
   context you weren't given?

4. Misleading plausibility -- Would a reader likely believe the wrong
   version as true, or would they notice something is off and question
   it? A fluent, believable wrong answer is more dangerous than an
   obviously broken one.
